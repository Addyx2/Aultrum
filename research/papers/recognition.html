Recognition that produces quality.

How incentive architecture — when gated by measured care quality — generates retention, quality, and purpose simultaneously.

Fifty-three percent of social care workers considered leaving in the past twelve months (NCFE, 2026). The industry response has been predictable: match pay, reduce caseloads, subsidise travel. These are necessary interventions. They are not sufficient.

The reason is not that pay is irrelevant. It is that pay is a hygiene factor — necessary to prevent exit, but incapable of producing the conditions under which quality care is sustainable. What does produce those conditions is recognition: the lived experience of being valued for the quality of the work, not just the hours of presence.

This paper proposes a different architecture: incentives gated by quality signals. Not "you showed up" — but "you were on time, the client praised you, your skill set matched the need." That architecture is what Peak is designed to test.

The retention mechanism is not what the industry thinks it is

The most commonly cited reason for care worker departure is pay — 35.2% of respondents in the 2024 UK workforce survey cited better pay elsewhere as their reason for leaving. But when providers actually invest in recognition, supervision, and wellbeing support alongside competitive pay, those measures are rated as "very effective" at retaining staff (Edge Hill University / Skills for Care, 2026).

The contradiction dissolves when you separate two things: pay is the floor; recognition is the ceiling. Most agencies are already paying at or above NLW. The marginal pound spent on recognition produces more retention than the marginal pound spent on another 50p/hour — because recognition changes the emotional relationship with the work.

Karaferis et al. (2022) found that recognition by seniors was one of the most important motivating factors among healthcare professionals, directly associated with organisational achievement and engagement at work. "Officially recognising best performance is suggested" — not as a perk, but as a structural driver of engagement.

The quality-recognition loop

Here is the causal chain the evidence supports:

THEORY: RECOGNITION → QUALITY → PURPOSE ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━ recognition (extrinsic, visible) → autonomous motivation (intrinsic, internal) → proactive behaviour (engagement, initiative) → quality of care (measurable outcomes) → client satisfaction (praise, trust, continuity) → purpose (why the carer stays) ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━ Grounded in: van den Heuvel et al. 2022 (PMC8876425) Wang et al. 2025 (J Nurs Res 33(1):e376) IHI CareFront programme 2025

Van den Heuvel et al.'s systematic review found that autonomous (intrinsic) motivation was associated with proactive behaviour, which was in turn associated with perceived quality of care. Critically, they concluded that "incentive-based quality improvements need to be complemented with aspects that stimulate autonomous motivation" — meaning that rewards alone are insufficient, but rewards that are quality-gated produce the intrinsic motivation that holds quality in place.

Wang et al. (2025) demonstrated that work engagement and job satisfaction fully mediate the relationship between perceived staffing and quality of care. Engaged staff provide better care; engaged staff stay. The loop is self-reinforcing — but only when something triggers the initial engagement.

What current systems get wrong

Agencies fund incentives. CQC assesses quality. The two systems operate in separate universes. An agency can spend £50,000/year on staff perks and bonuses and have no way to demonstrate that this spend produces measurably better care. A CQC inspector can identify quality concerns but has no mechanism to connect them to the incentive architecture that might address them.

The EU Framework of Action on Retention and Recruitment (2025, EPSU/Social Employers) calls for "valuing existing staff" as a formal priority area — alongside education, work-life balance, and occupational safety. But "valuing" remains unmeasured. There is no framework that takes a care worker's quality signals and uses them to structure the recognition they receive.

This is the gap Peak is designed to fill.

The Layers model: measuring what recognition is earned

The Aultrum Layers framework — Shift Readiness, Rapport, Competence, Compliance, Satisfaction, Purpose — provides the first architecture where incentive spend is gated by measured care quality.

In Peak's v1 design (P2, October 2026), two quality qualifiers go live:

The commercial consequence is significant: agency incentive spend becomes a documented investment in care quality, not an unmeasured staff perk. For CQC and Integrated Care Systems, this produces the quality evidence they need. For the agency CFO, it produces the retention economics they need. For the carer, it produces the recognition that produces purpose.

The performance measurement insight

A 2025 study of primary healthcare workers in China (PMC12198835) found a striking result: when performance measurement included both volume and quality of care, intrinsic motivation was significantly higher (β=0.61, p<0.05) than when measurement was volume-only. When incentives targeted groups rather than individuals, the effect was even stronger (β=1.04, p<0.05).

This directly validates Peak's architecture. Peak does not reward individual output volume. It rewards quality signals (readiness, rapport) at the individual level but within the group context of the agency team. The China study suggests this dual structure — quality-measured, group-contextualised — is the architecture most likely to produce autonomous motivation rather than the perverse gaming that volume-based incentives produce.

Pilot design: what Peak intends to test

The P2–P3 phase (October–November 2026) will instrument 3–5 agencies and 150+ carers with the qualifier engine. The study design:

Retention delta: 90-day voluntary departure rate pre- and post-Peak deployment. Primary outcome: >20% reduction in 90-day departure rate.

Quality scores: Rapport and Shift Readiness qualifier scores tracked over time. Secondary outcome: correlation between qualifier scores and client-reported satisfaction.

Purpose measure: Carer-reported purpose (adapted from the Work and Wellbeing Questionnaire) at baseline, 45 days, and 90 days. Tertiary outcome: measurable increase in intrinsic motivation scores among active participants.

Cost per retained carer: Agency Peak spend vs. the replacement cost of a departed carer (Skills for Care estimates £2,000–£5,000). Primary economic outcome: Peak cost <15% of the replacement cost it prevents.

Implications for commissioning

If the pilot evidence supports the thesis, the policy implication is significant: Integrated Care Systems could fund quality-gated recognition as a commissioning tool, not just a staff perk. The EU Framework (2025) already identifies "valuing existing staff" as a formal priority. The missing piece is a measurement framework that ties that value to quality outcomes. Peak's qualifier engine provides it.

Skills for Care has argued that "recognition should extend to the adult social care workforce, who carry out skilled work to provide care and support for people with increasingly complex care needs" (parliamentary evidence, RTR0034). This paper argues that recognition, when structured as a quality signal rather than a discretionary bonus, does exactly that: it makes care quality visible, measurable, and rewarding — for the carer, the agency, and the people they care for.

Limitations and honesty

This is a pre-pilot paper. The architecture is grounded in peer-reviewed evidence on motivation, recognition, and quality in healthcare; the causal claims about Peak's specific qualifier engine are to be tested. The China PBP study is from primary care, not social care. The IHI CareFront programme is US-based and hospital-context. The authors do not claim the findings generalise without UK social care validation — which is precisely what the P2–P3 pilot is designed to produce.
